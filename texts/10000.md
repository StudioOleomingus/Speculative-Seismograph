# **Tripti, Dhruvi**

**Untitled.Tripti.Dhruvi**

For several millennia, humans have altered cows through domestication and selective breeding. Wild aurochs were gradually transformed into specialized dairy and beef breeds designed for human consumption. Dairy cows today produce more than four times the amount of milk compared to cows in the 1950s, beef production systems have also been optimized for rapid growth and high yields. Their **bodies, genetics, and reproduction have been shaped to serve systems of efficiency, mass production, and global food demand*.***

This project imagines a speculative future where this long history of manipulation triggers an unexpected biological response. **A global genetic mutation spreads through cattle populations, shrinking all cows to pocket-sized beings.** What was once a large agricultural animal becomes a fragile miniature species that fits in the palm of a hand. Unable to produce milk in quantities useful for human consumption and no longer viable for meat production, losing their industrial value.

They begin to exist in a different relationship with humans, kept inside small enclosures, glass cases, and protective habitats to prevent accidental harm, even from predators as small as rodents. As their utility disappears, their symbolic and sacred value intensifies.

However, **the last original-sized cow remains**.

**Governments, dairy corporations, religious institutions, and cultural organizations collaborate to organize the** **final milking ceremony**, broadcast internationally. The milk collected during this event is treated as a rare and irreplaceable substance. It is auctioned to an exclusive group of buyers  (multimillionaires, billionaires, museums, dairy cooperatives, and powerful  
institutions) turning what an everyday commodity into a luxury artifact and cultural relic. The final glass is auctioned to a billionaire, whose spoiled children insist on flavouring the last remaining milk so that it tastes like orange juice.

After the ceremony, the cow is placed in a **cryogenic** **preservation chamber**, suspended between life and death. The preservation is carried out in the hope that this last original-sized cow can one day be used to study and potentially reverse the mutation. Carefully maintained but no longer part of a living ecosystem, she becomes both a biological specimen and monument. 

This project draws attention to the normalization of exploitation within industrial food systems, where animal bodies are engineered purely for output. It also reflects on how access to **“natural”** food increasingly becomes a class privilege. Finally, it questions how ecological loss is easily transformed into spectacle, ritual, and commodity instead of addressing its systemic causes.
