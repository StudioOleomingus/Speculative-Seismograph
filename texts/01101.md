# **The Afterlife of info\_Reet**

**Untitled.Reet**

**What if your toilet knew more about you than your family, your friends, or your browser history?** 

Every time someone flushes a toilet, the system learns a little more about how the citizens think. Information is treated like food. People gorge on articles, reels, memes, podcasts, and opinion threads, and their bodies digest it all. What remains is **cognitive waste**: a physical residue that reveals exactly what kind of information a person consumed. Every public toilet is equipped with sensors and connected to a national monitoring network that analyses the waste when it is flushed and generates an **Intellectual Health Report**. A small receipt might read: *“User consumed 23 reels, 5 memes, 1 opinion article. Cognitive fiber critically low. Suggestion: Try reading something longer than a caption.”*

Beneath the city, sewage systems have been upgraded into massive infrastructures that function as both treatment plants and data centers. Streams of cognitive waste move through underground pipelines where the country’s most valued workers operate: **Cognitive Scavengers**. Once the most disrespected form of labor, these workers are now celebrated as heroes. Government intelligence agencies hire them to descend into the pipes and recover fragments of thought machines fail to detect, tracing patterns from the waste of politicians, scientists, journalists, and activists, especially when certain pipelines attract the attention of national security agencies, working undercover quite literally beneath the city.

Occasionally, some samples are considered historically significant and are preserved in the **National Museum of Intellectual Waste**. Inside glass jars sit the cognitive remains of famous figures. Visitors gather around exhibits such as *“Residual Thought Fragments of a Nobel Prize winning scientist before announcing a breakthrough.”* School children on field visits wander through aisles of preserved thought, studying the strange afterlife of ideas that were once simply flushed away.
