# **0111**

**Untitled.Janavi.Sarvesh**

Why should your extra work simply disappear at the end of the day when it can be stored and counted?

In this imagined system, overtime is no longer something informal or unnoticed. Every extra hour a worker spends at their job can now be deposited into a personal **Overtime Bank**. Offices and factories have installed special machines that look exactly like ATMs, but instead of dispensing money, these machines collect time. After finishing their shift, employees simply place their finger on the machine’s sensor to deposit their overtime hours. The screen then displays polite messages such as “Thank you for your contribution” or “Overtime successfully deposited,” making the process feel official and rewarding. 

Over time, workers can see their overtime balance increasing, creating a visible record of dedication and productivity. 

The **Overtime Bank Protocol** was introduced as a modern solution for improving workplace productivity. Companies believed that if overtime was tracked like money, workers would feel proud of their growing balance of extra hours.

The machine collects fingerprints to confirm each deposit. A small screen displays messages and keeps track of how much overtime has been contributed. Workers are encouraged to keep adding more hours to their accounts.

However, there is one important rule in the system.

Once time is deposited into the Overtime Bank, it **cannot be withdrawn**.

Those hours do not return as rest, payment, or free time. They simply remain recorded as proof of loyalty and commitment to the company. Slowly, the machine fills with thousands of hours donated by workers who keep believing their effort will matter.

Although this idea sounds exaggerated, it reflects a reality that already exists in many workplaces today. Employees often work late, answer messages after office hours, and sacrifice personal time without receiving equal compensation or rest.

The **Overtime ATM Machine** turns this invisible system into a visible object. By showing overtime as something that workers physically deposit into a machine, the artifact highlights how labor can be recorded and praised while offering little real return.

This speculation asks an important question:

**What if overtime was treated like a bank deposit carefully recorded and appreciated but never returned to the worker who gave it?**

Through this fictional system, the artifact invites viewers to reflect on how modern work culture values productivity, dedication, and the true cost of human time.
