# **VIPpass\_Ishan How?**

**Untitled.Ishan**

EXHIBIT 07: *Maharashtra State BPL Ration Card. Recovered from the Hadapsar Municipal Archive, circa 2092\. National Museum of Post-Independence India, Mumbai.*  
\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_

The card you're looking at is about a century old.

It was issued by the Office of the Tehsildar in 2003, Pune, Maharashtra. The person in the photograph is Abani Singh. He was the head of his household — a family of 5, living in Galli number 4, Dattanagar, Baragram. The card entitled him to fifteen kilograms of rice and two kilograms of sugar every month, at subsidised prices. It's laminated, bears a government seal, a card number, an entitlement table, and the signature of a state official.

By the time you're reading this, it's 2094\. **What strikes historians about this object, then, is not what it says — but the fact that it needed to exist at all.** A century ago, whether a family ate this month depended on whether this rectangle of plastic was physically in their possession. **That the state's entire relationship with a person — their identity, their need, their entitlement — could be laminated and lost in a pocket.** Historians who have studied this card have arrived at a consistent interpretation. **They've described it as a priority access credential.** They note the photograph as evidence that identity verification was central to the privilege system of the era. The entitlement table is interpreted as a schedule of benefits made available to verified, state-approved citizens. This is being taught in school.

They're not lying, nor are they being careless. They are simply reading the object in the only language available to them—a language in which a government-issued document with your photograph and an official stamp means one thing: the state chose you.  
What's hidden — because the archive that would tell them has mostly rotted or been lost — is that to hold this card in 2003 meant to formally be classified as poor. BPL was not a distinction the state made to honour you. It was a threshold: your household income had been measured and found insufficient. The card was proof of that insufficiency. You carried it to a ration shop, handed it to a man behind a counter, and waited while he recorded how much of the monthly allowance you had used.

Getting it required proof of address, a cooperative local official, and the patience to return to the same government office week after week. Millions who were genuinely poorer held no card at all — too mobile, too undocumented, too invisible even for this system to reach. Being seen by the state was already a privilege. 

**The document of deprivation was also a document of partial inclusion.**
