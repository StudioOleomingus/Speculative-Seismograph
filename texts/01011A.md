







#Untitled.01011A
*By Tanmay & Milli*

Why should humans move freely across every landscape when ecosystems themselves cannot?

Different environments carry unique bacteria, microbes, and biological balances. Yet human mobility ignores these invisible systems, crossing borders without adapting to the living conditions of each ecosystem.

In this speculative world, crossing a border is no longer a political procedure but a biological one. Entry into a new territory requires the human body to adapt to the microbial environment of that ecosystem. If the body cannot biologically coexist with the local bacteria, the border simply will not allow passage.

The **Ecological Border Adaptation Protocol** links human mobility directly to ecological compatibility. At every border checkpoint, travelers must consume a sequence of specially engineered adaptation pills designed to prepare the body for a new microbial environment.

After consuming the pills, travelers are placed inside **Ecological Adaptation Chambers** located at the border. These chambers simulate the biological conditions of the destination ecosystem. Over a period of **three days**, the pills gradually help the body adjust to unfamiliar bacteria, microorganisms, and environmental conditions.

Only after this adaptation period can a traveler safely enter the new territory.Without this preparation, human bodies risk contaminating fragile ecosystems or becoming vulnerable to foreign microbes themselves. The border therefore functions not as a wall but as a **biological filter**, ensuring that bodies entering an ecosystem are capable of existing within its microbial balance.

The adaptation chamber turns border crossing into a waiting ritual — a moment where humans must pause and biologically negotiate with the environment before entering it. Instead of presenting passports or visas alone, travelers must demonstrate that their bodies are capable of coexisting with the living systems of the land.

This speculation emerges from growing concerns about ecological fragility, microbial ecosystems, and the invisible biological exchanges that accompany global movement. As ecosystems become increasingly sensitive to disturbance, technological and biotechnological systems begin to regulate how bodies interact with environments.

But the idea exaggerates a deeper question .already present today:**What if ecosystems had the authority to control human movement rather than governments?**

By imagining borders as biological thresholds, the project challenges the assumption that humans can freely inhabit any landscape without consequence. It asks us to reconsider mobility not as a right granted by nations, but as a negotiation with the living systems that make environments possible.
